A reconstruction · New York to Gibraltar · 7 Nov – 13 Dec 1872

The Mary Celeste,
1872

Ten people left a sound brigantine somewhere off the Azores and were never seen again. This monograph separates what the 1872–73 record actually says from 150 years of fiction, rebuilds the voyage on a chart, and gives a reasoned verdict on the morning of 25 November.

SUMMARYWhere the case stands

The Mary Celeste sailed from New York for Genoa on 7 November 1872. She carried 1,701 barrels of commercial alcohol, Captain Benjamin Spooner Briggs, his wife Sarah, their two-year-old daughter Sophia, and seven crew. Her last recorded position is a slate entry at 8 a.m. on 25 November, six miles from the eastern point of Santa María in the Azores. On the afternoon of 4 December the Nova Scotian brigantine Dei Gratia found her about 377 nautical miles further east, sailing herself under two headsails, with nobody aboard and the ship's boat gone.

Established

  • The ship was sound: no collision, no fire, no explosion, no blood.
  • The boat was launched deliberately.
  • The chronometer, sextant, navigation book and register left with the people.
  • Clothing, pipes, valuables and six months' food stayed aboard.

Probable

  • She was abandoned in the forenoon of 25 November, in sight of Santa María.
  • The crew left because they believed she was making water. The sounding gear lay by a dismantled pump.
  • The boat was lost in the gale that followed the morning lull.

Rejected

  • Mutiny and murder (Solly-Flood, 1873).
  • Conspiracy with the Dei Gratia (Wilkins, Rakitin).
  • Every "survivor" account: Fosdyk, Pemberton/Keating, Hamel, Lucy.
  • Piracy, seaquake, sea monster.

How far from finished: the reconstruction is complete as far as the published and online record allows. Three things could still move the verdict. None is online. They are the Gibraltar registrar's notes of evidence, Solly-Flood's report of 22 January 1873, and a Spanish press check of the "two rafts" reported from Asturias in May 1873. The open questions section has the detail.

METHODSources and how they are graded

The story was retold and embroidered almost from the start. So every statement here carries a source, and the sources are graded. The rule from the Courland work applies: the contemporary document beats the later book, and a later book counts only for what it quotes.

  • A Primary 1872–73. Austin's survey, Portunato's dive affidavit, Patron's analysis, the crew list, Briggs's and Sarah's letters, Deveau's depositions as quoted verbatim, the Gibraltar Chronicle, and the US Treasury circular.
  • B Scholarly secondary that prints the documents. Fay (1942), Kimball (1942), Hastings (1972), Begg (2005), Hicks (2004), MacGregor (2007).
  • C Later recollection, or a secondary source with errors. Deveau's obituary (1912), Mrs Richardson (1902), Priscilla Shelton (1936), Wilkins (1947).
  • X Fiction or fabrication. Doyle (1884), Fosdyk (1913), Keating (1929), and the deathbed "survivors".

The Gibraltar court produced no verbatim transcript. In 1973 the US National Archives confirmed there was none, and the registrar holds only notes of evidence. Every "Q and A" version of the hearing is therefore partly a reconstruction. The dialogue in the Evernote dossier is one of these; see its assessment.

I · THE SHIPFrom Amazon to Mary Celeste

Joshua Dewis built her at Spencer's Island, on the Bay of Fundy. The keel was laid in 1860 and she was launched in May 1861; one 1914 recollection says July. On 10 June 1861 she was registered at Parrsboro as the Amazon, official number 37671. Her first master, Robert McLellan, was the son of one of the owners. He fell ill and died ashore on 19 June 1861. He did not go overboard, as Rakitin has it. In October 1867 she went ashore at Big Glace Bay on Cape Breton. She was sold as a wreck, refloated, and in 1868 bought at a New York salvage sale by Richard W. Haines for $1,750. On 31 December 1868 she was re-registered at New York as the Mary Celeste, Register No. 485.

The 1872 rebuild was substantial. The poop was extended over all and she got new transoms and several new timbers, part-new knightheads, stem and stern, and new bends and topsides. The topsail was probably split into upper and lower at this time. The cost is given as $10,000 in one source and $14,000 in another. Deveau found the hull "nearly new". This matters twice over. Fresh plank work explains the shallow strips found missing from both bows, as US Navy Captain Shufeldt said in 1873. And a rebuilt hold full of shavings and dust is a plausible reason for pumps that could not be trusted.

Ownership on the day she sailed

Register No. 22 of 29 October 1872 divided her into twenty-fourths. James H. Winchester held 12, Sylvester Goodwin 2, Daniel T. Samson 2, and Benjamin S. Briggs 8. Briggs was therefore part-owner of a third of the vessel, and he took his wife and child in her. That fact alone weighs heavily against every fraud theory.

The character evidence on the crew

Solly-Flood's mutiny theory rested on the four German seamen. In April 1873 T. A. Nickelsen of Utersum, on the island of Föhr, wrote to Consul Sprague. He knew three of the five crewmen personally, he said, as "peaceable and first-class sailors". Sprague forwarded the letter to Washington on 4 April 1873 (despatch No. 142). He commented that it "further diminishes the possibility that any violence was committed on board". In 1885 the Imperial German consul on Föhr wrote that two of the men had been his own school comrades. A

What the figures say

  • Santa María fix to the place she was found: 377 nautical miles by Deveau's dead reckoning (38°20′N 17°15′W). By Morehouse's chronometer longitude (17°37′W) it is 360 miles.
  • Time adrift, from 08:00 on 25 November to about 14:00 on 4 December: 222 hours. That gives an average of 1.7 knots. Deveau saw her "going steadily from 1½ knots to 2 knots" when sighted. A derelict starting on the 25th fits his observation without any human help.
  • Where the Dei Gratia was at 08:00 on 25 November. The documented points are her departure (15 Nov), the speaking at 40°55′N 66°W (19 Nov), and her daily runs of 200, 162, 150 and 90 miles for 1–4 December. From these she was in mid-ocean near 41°N 46°W, about 1,000 nautical miles west of the Mary Celeste. She could not have been at any rendezvous off Santa María.
  • The log itself is short of the island. From noon on the 24th the log has her running at 8–9 knots, east by south. At that speed she should have been abreast of Santa María well before dawn. Instead the island was sighted ahead, bearing ESE, at 5 a.m. Only 111 miles separate the noon fix from the 8 a.m. fix, which is 5.6 knots over 20 hours. Either her speed was overstated or the noon longitude was wrong. Anne MacGregor's chronometer-error argument (2007) predicts exactly this.

The brigantine sails herself east-north-east at under two knots, with the jib and fore-topmast staysail drawing. The foresail and upper fore-topsail blow out. Rain and spray come in through the open skylight and hatches; that is the water Deveau found between decks.

The 5 a.m. and 8 a.m. slate entries agree with each other. A ship on E by S at about 8 knots sees Santa María bearing ESE at dawn. Three hours later she has the island's eastern point SSW of her, six miles off, having passed along its northern shore. The slate is internally consistent, so nothing suggests it was fabricated.

VI · GIBRALTARA salvage case that became a murder hunt

  • 12 December 1872. The Dei Gratia anchored at Gibraltar. 13 December, morning: the Mary Celeste came in after a gale in the Strait, during which Deveau had laid to under Ceuta. She was sailed in, not towed, whatever the Treasury circular says.
  • 17–18 December. The vessel was arrested "as found derelict", and the hearing opened before Cochrane with Deveau as the first witness. Sources differ by one day on the opening date.
  • 23 December. Austin's survey and Portunato's dive, with Vecchio and Solly-Flood present.
  • 23 December. Deveau sailed for Genoa in command of the Dei Gratia, and Morehouse stayed behind. On 31 January Cochrane called this "most reprehensible" and recalled Deveau, who testified again on 4 March.
  • 7 January 1873. Solly-Flood made a further inspection with four Royal Navy captains and Colonel Laffan, RE. They agreed the bow damage was intentional.
  • 22 January. Solly-Flood reported to the Board of Trade. His theory was that the crew got at the alcohol, "in the fury of drunkenness murdered the Master", his wife, child and mate, and cut the bows to fake a collision. The Board of Trade later destroyed its file.
  • 29–31 January. Winchester testified. He knew Briggs as a courageous officer "who would not … desert his ship except to save his life".
  • Late February. Shufeldt reported for Sprague: the crew abandoned her "probably in a moment of panic", and the bow marks were the work of the sea.
  • Release. The Mary Celeste was released on bond in late February, on the 25th or the 28th. On 10 March she sailed for Genoa under Captain George W. Blatchford and arrived on 21 March.
  • 14 March 1873: the award. The Gibraltar Chronicle of 15 March valued the vessel at $5,700 and the cargo at $36,943, $42,643 in all. The award was £1,700, about $8,300 and roughly a fifth of that value. Court costs, Patron's analysis among them, were charged against it. Cochrane's remarks were pointedly cool towards the salvors.
  • 24–25 March. The US Treasury circular, signed by Secretary W. A. Richardson, adopted Solly-Flood's theory and asked collectors to watch for the crew.

What Deveau did wrong, and why it is not conspiracy

Wilkins builds his case on Deveau's lapses. Deveau rubbed out the slate when he first used it. He wrote up the log from memory after arrival, and his entry for 6 December wrongly has Morehouse coming aboard. He also left the jurisdiction. All of this is in the record. All of it is the conduct of a mate who was "so tired I can hardly tell what I am made of" and who wanted his salvage settled. None of it moves the Dei Gratia 1,000 miles east on the morning of 25 November.

VII · THEORIESEach theory tested against the hard facts

A theory has to explain all of these at once. The boat went with instruments and papers. Personal effects and food stayed. There was no violence and no damage. The well had just been sounded. Land was six miles off. And the Dei Gratia was about 1,000 miles away at the time.

Notes on the strongest and the weakest

  • Fumes. Andrea Sella's 2006 UCL model and the 2026 Manchester test (Rowbotham and Mair) both show that an alcohol-vapour deflagration can lift hatches without scorching anything. So the lack of burn marks does not rule it out. Against it: Austin looked for exactly this and found nothing, and the main hatch was still secured. The open fore hatch and lazarette fit someone venting the hold, not a blast blowing hatches off. Fumes work best as an aggravating factor in a decision driven by the water question.
  • Waterspout. It needs no human error, but it needs weather nobody recorded. The Azores stations show a front, not spouts.
  • Seaquake. The Azores meteorological service told Fay in 1940 that no earthquake was registered in the station books or the island papers for those days. A
  • Mutiny. Patron's analysis, the untouched valuables, the Föhr testimonials and Shufeldt's survey all tell against it. The alcohol was undrinkable commercial spirit, and there was no wine or beer aboard.
  • Conspiracy with the finders. It fails on distance. At the hour the slate stops, the Dei Gratia was a week's sailing to the west. She sailed eight days after the Mary Celeste and met her 377 miles beyond the island. A rendezvous needs the Mary Celeste to keep station for a week with a crew aboard and then vanish without trace, and still nobody turned up in the Azores, Portugal or anywhere else.

VIII · VERDICTWhat most probably happened

On the night of 24 November the Mary Celeste ran east by south through rain and a fresh westerly, with royals and topgallants taken in. She was further west than her reckoning showed, and at dawn Santa María rose ahead of her, not astern. By 8 a.m. she had rounded its northern shore and was six miles off the eastern point. The wind fell away to the morning lull that the Azores stations recorded.

At some point in the forenoon a reason to doubt the ship arose. The most likely candidate is the water. She was newly rebuilt and had probably carried coal before, so her pumps could be choked with dust and shavings. One pump was found with its box drawn so a rod could be lowered into the well, and the rod lay beside it. A sounding taken that way after a heavy night may well have looked alarming. Leaking red-oak barrels and a smell of spirit in a hold that had been battened down for eighteen days would have added to the fear. The fore hatch and lazarette were opened.

With land six miles off in light air, a cautious master with his wife and child aboard took the course that looked safe. He put everyone in the yawl with the chronometer, sextant, navigation book and register, meaning either to stand by or to make Santa María. Whether the main peak halyard served as a painter, as Fay thought, cannot be proved. The front came through in the afternoon, the wind went round to the north-west at gale force, and an open boat with ten people in it did not survive. The brigantine, with her jib and fore-topmast staysail drawing, sailed on alone for nine days.

Confidence: the time and place of abandonment are B, probable. The cause is C: it is the best fit to the evidence, and it cannot be proved without the boat or a body. A parallel worth knowing: Briggs's brother, Captain Oliver Briggs, lost the Julia A. Hallock in the Bay of Biscay on 8 January 1873. Her pumps were choked with coal dust and all hands but one were lost.

The essay's real merit is the question in its title. And the answer to "where was she going?" is clear from the chart: she was going nowhere. She was drifting at 1.7 knots, wherever the wind put her.

2 · Keating, The Great Mary Celeste Hoax (London: Heath Cranton, 1929): 237 page photographs plus OCR

The photographs make a complete copy, from frontispiece to index. The OCR in mc_hoax.docx is clean in the body text and garbled in the front-matter contents. As history the book is worthless, and that is useful in itself. It shows how a fabrication is built:

  • It "quotes" the Minutes of Evidence and makes them say the opposite of the record: a chase lasting a day, a German steamer, both boats in their chocks, three cups of warm tea, a boiled chicken on the stove, a cat on the locker, a seaman called "Whelligan".
  • It replaces the sworn crew list with its own: Hullock, Dossell, Moffat, Manning, Hawley, Sanson, Venholdt and Pemberton. It drops Sophia ("nor a child").
  • It invents a builder ("Henry Matthew Colfax" of Parrsborough) and a past name, "Mary Sellars", which Wilkins then repeated.
  • Its plates (deck plan, cabin plans, "The Canaries Current") are well drawn and serve the fiction. The deck plan shows two boats.

3 · Wilkins, New Light on the Most Famous of All Sea Mysteries (Girard, Kansas: Haldeman-Julius, 1947)

The booklet is unreliable as an argument and still valuable as a quarry, because it prints texts at length:

  • Useful: Deveau's answers on the boat, pumps, galley and cabin, matching the depositions in Fay. Patron's analysis in full. Sprague's letters, including the Savoy sword. The 1931 replies from the State Department and the Board of Trade showing that the log and the British file were lost or destroyed. Priscilla Richardson Shelton's letter of 16 March 1936. The exposure of a forged "despatch No. 139" naming a "Henry Bilson".
  • Wrong: built at Bath, Maine. Renamed "Mary Sellars" and then owned by the French. Pisani made "Marshal". The cuts placed "below the waterline", where Austin has them 2–3 ft above. The ship "went down off Cuba in a hurricane" in 1885; she was deliberately wrecked off Haiti. Morehouse sailing as "Boyce" is Fosdyk's name for the Dei Gratia's master, which Wilkins takes as evidence.
  • Method: it treats every clerical lapse by Deveau as a cover-up, and it never confronts the distance between the two ships.

4 · The Evernote dossier (Mary_Celeste.pdf, pp. 10–181)

This is a working collection. It holds real primaries alongside reconstructions, and they need to be kept apart:

  • Primary, usable: the crew list facsimile; Briggs's letter of 3 Nov 1872; Sarah's of 7 Nov; Deveau's letter to his wife of 14 Dec 1872; Austin's survey, ¶1–65; Portunato's affidavit; Patron's report; Register No. 22; the Atlantic Mutual adjustment; the Gibraltar inventory of effects (10 Mar 1873); the Nickelsen and Föhr letters; the Azores weather record; the Berwick Register clippings of 1912, 1915, 1934 and 1938.
  • Dramatised: the question-and-answer "statements" of Deveau, Morehouse, Wright, Anderson, Lund and Winchester. Real deposition sentences are woven into invented exchanges. Flood calls Johnson "Russian"; the judge muses aloud. The Q&A form cannot be quoted as testimony. Only sentences that also appear in Fay, Kimball or Wilkins can.
  • A lead, unverified: the Liverpool Daily Albion, 16 May 1873. A Madrid telegram reported that fishermen at "Baudus" in Asturias, probably Candás, found two rafts, one carrying a body under an "American" flag and one with five decomposed bodies. Keating already used it ("The Mary Celeste's rafts", p. 50). A yawl is not a raft, and May is late. It is worth one Spanish newspaper search, and that is all.
